Community Organization

Designing for Discovery: From Feed to Hubs

Role Community UX Designer, Lore
Timeline Add timeline
Tools Figma, FigJam

The problem

Lore launched with a single reverse-chronological feed. Open the app and you’d scroll past a sleep-tips post, then a one-word reflection on peace, then a video about making friends, then a story about an ice bath — all with equal weight, in whatever order they’d been posted.

That’s fine when the goal is keeping people scrolling. It’s the wrong model when the goal is helping someone feel less alone in something specific. A user struggling with sleep had no way to find the other people talking about sleep. They could see that people were reflecting, but not where their reflection belonged.

New users felt this hardest — they’d land on a feed of strangers’ unrelated posts, find nothing that spoke to them, and leave.

The hypothesis

We could have improved ranking. But better ranking still optimizes for what to show next, not for helping someone find where they fit — and belonging was the actual mission.

Therefore we asked a narrower question: could we help people find relevance — a topic, a recurring set of voices, a place — before we tried to help them find content.

The intervention

We built organic hubs on top of existing autolinking. As posts were written, the system auto-tagged them against topics like #Meditation, #Breathing, #Overwhelm — no manual categorizing required. A post could carry more than one tag and route into more than one hub, so a meditation post that touched on anxiety could surface in both places.

Hubs weren’t groups you joined. They were lenses onto the same content — letting someone filter “Finding Strength in Meditation” out of the general stream and into a thread with people circling the same thing.

What worked

Super users adopted hubs almost immediately. They used the tag filters to track recurring topics, recognize familiar names, and reply with intention instead of posting into the void. The underlying theory held: given a way to find context, people reciprocated.

What didn’t

It didn’t reach the users who needed it most. A new or quiet user still had to know a hub existed, know to tap into it, and post with no signal anyone would respond. Discovery still rewarded people who were already active and confident — the same users who didn’t need help finding belonging in the first place.

Autolinking solved where content goes. It did nothing for who shows up.

Learning

Categorization is an organizing tool, not an onboarding tool. Hubs gave structure to conversations that already had momentum, but structure alone can’t create someone’s first reason to speak. That gap — the cold start, the blank-page moment — needed a different kind of intervention, which is where the next round of experiments picked up.
