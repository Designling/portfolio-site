Simplifying Entry

Removing the decision from Lore’s front door for a 65+ user base.

Role UX Designer
Timeline One week
Tools Lyssna, Figma

The Problem

A new client brought us a 65+ population, and we started sending welcome emails to thousands of people who had never heard of us.

Those emails were personalized. Name, plan, the works.

To this audience, a company that knows your name is a company that already has your file. Users arrived at the front door certain they had an account and clicked Sign in.

They didn’t have accounts. They hit a wall and called customer care.

These weren’t ordinary support calls. Before an agent could help, they had to talk the caller out of a belief about their own history with us — a slow, uncomfortable conversation that happened before any actual support began.

We assumed the copy caused it and audited the marketing emails first. Nothing in them claimed an account existed. The belief wasn’t something we said. It was something the email’s existence implied.

The Bet

The screen offered three doors: Sign in, Learn more, Create account.

That asked every arriving user to correctly classify themselves before entering. But this population classified themselves wrong, confidently — and no amount of label-tuning fixes a confident wrong answer.

So we stopped asking.

One email field. One button: Continue to Lore.

  • Email on file → sign-in
  • Email not on file → account creation

The system already knew which kind of user this was. No reason to make the user guess.

As a new or returning user, I need to get into Lore without having to make a decision about what I am.

What It Cost Us

Typo risk. Fat-finger your address as a returning user and you land in account creation.

Autofill does most of the typing on that screen, so it’s rarer in practice than on paper. And it fails into territory care already knows — they merge accounts routinely, since users move between clients. The worst case had a well-worn fix, not a dead end.

An unfamiliar pattern for established users. Lore isn’t an app people sign into constantly. The screen has to work best for the first time, not the fiftieth. Familiarity was the cheaper thing to trade.

The Test

A first-click test against the old screen. 30 participants, one day, $60.

Imagine you received an email and you just clicked a button that said “I’m ready to sign up.” Then, this screen appeared. Where would you click first?

Old design: 4 participants clicked a target that wouldn’t get them in. New design: 1.

Confidence came in slightly lower on the new screen. Only slightly, and it tracks — a single button that doesn’t announce where it leads gives people less to confirm their choice against. Not enough to stop on.

At n=30 this was directional, not conclusive. It pointed the same way the call logs already did, which was enough to ship on.

The Outcome

Zero customer care calls about this issue across the next several months of new onboardings.

The category didn’t shrink. It closed.
