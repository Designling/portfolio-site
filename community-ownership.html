Community Ownership

Designing for Participation: Plants, and the Search for a Reason to Show Up

Role Community UX Designer, Lore
Timeline Add timeline
Tools Figma, FigJam

The problem

Hubs gave people a place to find relevant conversation. They didn’t give people a reason to feel like that place was theirs. Even inside a hub, participation felt transactional — post, maybe get a reply, move on.

Round 1: A plant for every theme

Hypothesis. If people could see their own investment in something they cared about, they might feel ownership over it — not the community’s ownership, their ownership.

Intervention. Themes sat above hubs in the hierarchy — Mental, Diet, Family, Goals, Hard Stuff, Financial, Medication, Exercise — with hubs like #Meditation or #Breathing living underneath. We gave each theme its own plant, but the plant wasn’t a group score. It was personal: it reflected whether you were engaging with that theme. Show up, and it grew. Stay away, and it got shelved — not punished, just quietly archived as something that wasn’t relevant to you right now.

What happened. The idea landed well with the squad — it made caring about something visible without making it a leaderboard. But tracking a personal plant state for every user, across every theme, was a much heavier build than a single shared indicator. We lost our frontend engineer mid-project, and the system was too complex to ship responsibly at that point.

We shelved it ourselves. Not because the idea was wrong, but because shipping it well would have meant cutting corners on something meant to feel personal. Good ideas still have to survive reality — and letting go of one is a design decision, not a failure of one.

Round 2: One plant per person

Hypothesis. Therefore we asked: what’s the smallest version of personal ownership we could still ship — one plant per person, reflecting their own participation.

Intervention. Reading, commenting, or posting all watered it. The plant grew larger over time, eventually blooming — a lightweight, individual signal that was actually yours.

What worked. Participation rose. People hadn’t had anything like this before, and because the plant was genuinely theirs, they owned it.

What didn’t. Ownership worked almost too well. Some users started posting for the plant — to make it grow — rather than because they had something they actually wanted to reflect on. The plant had successfully become a reason to participate, but for some people it had quietly replaced the original reason to participate.

The signal was the same as before, but the mechanism behind it was different: engagement is not the same as connection — not because nobody felt ownership, but because ownership over a growth mechanic can motivate behavior all on its own, disconnected from whether the person had anything real to say.

Round 3: Giving people something to say

Problem. If participation could be triggered by a mechanic instead of a genuine impulse to reflect, the content itself needed a better source than “I want my plant to grow.” Some users also simply didn’t know what to share, mechanic aside.

Hypothesis. A shared daily prompt could give everyone a reason to post that had nothing to do with watering anything — a reason rooted in a real question, at a real moment, shared by everyone at once.

Intervention. We shipped a daily reflection channel — one prompt a day, the same for everyone. A prompt like “Share a memory associated with food that holds special meaning for you. How does it connect to your current relationship with food?” gives people permission, not just an opening.

What worked. Participation jumped again, but this time content got deeper — the prompt pulled people back toward reflecting on something specific rather than posting to hit a growth threshold.

What didn’t. The prompt shaped what people shared, but not consistently how. Tone and depth stayed uneven — some replies were vulnerable and specific, others stayed surface-level, and nothing in the product distinguished between them.

Learning

Three rounds, one throughline: personal ownership drives participation — sometimes more effectively than intended, to the point that the mechanic itself becomes the motivation. Daily reflection solved for genuine reason-to-post. It didn’t yet solve for quality of what got posted — which is exactly where the next intervention had to start.
